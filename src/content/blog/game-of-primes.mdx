## 02 — The Riemann Zeta Function

Now comes the interesting part: the Riemann Zeta Function.

The Riemann Zeta Function is an infinite series that looks something like this:

$$
\boxed{
\zeta(s)=1+\frac{1}{2^s}+\frac{1}{3^s}+\frac{1}{4^s}+\cdots
}
$$

At first glance, it looks like a never-ending addition of fractions. But there's something interesting about it. Depending on the value of $s$, this infinite series converges to different values. For example, when $s=2$, the entire infinite series converges to:

$$
\boxed{\zeta(2)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}}
$$

But Riemann took this function a step further. Instead of restricting $s$ to ordinary real numbers, he extended the function into the world of complex numbers.

## 03 — Enter the imaginary numbers

We usually understand numbers as points on a straight number line. But complex numbers introduce another dimension.

A complex number can be represented as:

$$
\boxed{s=\sigma+it}
$$

Here, $\sigma$ represents the real part, $t$ represents the coefficient of the imaginary component, and $i$ is the imaginary unit.

This means that instead of studying the Zeta Function along a single number line, we can study its behaviour across a two-dimensional complex plane.

And now comes the interesting part: the zeros of the Zeta Function.

Just like any other mathematical function, we can investigate the values at which its output becomes zero. These are called the zeros of the function.

Some of these are already understood and are called trivial zeros:

$$
\boxed{s=-2,-4,-6,-8,\ldots}
$$

The others, known as non-trivial zeros, lie within a region called the critical strip, where the real part of $s$ is between 0 and 1.

$$
\boxed{0<\operatorname{Re}(s)<1}
$$

Riemann proposed something extraordinary: what if every single non-trivial zero lies exactly on one vertical line, where the real part is always $1/2$?

That vertical line is called the critical line.

$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(s)=\frac12}
$$

And this is essentially what the Riemann Hypothesis is about.

## 04 — Okay, but why should I care?

Now you may ask, "What the actual fuck are you larping about, Abhinav?" I understand your frustration. Tbh, me being a DM failure, I can't speak much about math, but it's my blog and I will do whatever the fuck I want.

So, to put it simply, the whole point of this hypothesis is to understand the behaviour and distribution of prime numbers. The non-trivial zeros of the Riemann Zeta Function are connected to the fluctuations in prime distribution. This relationship is what makes this topic so interesting.

And finally, the most important thing to note, and also the most misunderstood part of the Riemann Hypothesis, is that we think it is about pinpointing the number of primes and stuff. No, it is not. There are functions and theorems to estimate the number of primes, like the Prime Number Theorem.

$$
\boxed{\pi(x)\sim\frac{x}{\ln x}}
$$

This theorem estimates the number of primes in a given range of numbers.

And the thing about this is that the Prime Number Theorem is not accurate enough to depend on. Yeah, it gives a good estimation, but not quite an accurate one.

For example, if we take one million, the formula estimates that there are around 72,382 primes below it. The actual number is 78,498. Not exact, but honestly, that's a pretty decent estimate.

So now we use the Riemann Zeta Function to understand and bound the error in this kind of estimation. And this is expressed as:

$$
\boxed{
\pi(x)=\operatorname{Li}(x)+O\left(\sqrt{x}\ln x\right)
}
$$

Here,

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Li}(x)=\int_2^x\frac{dt}{\ln t}
}
$$

is a more accurate estimate of prime counts, and the $O$ notation describes the scale of the error.

So, the Prime Number Theorem gives us the general trend, while RH tells us how tightly we can bound the error around that trend. And that's why a seemingly simple statement about a vertical line at $1/2$ has such massive implications for number theory.
